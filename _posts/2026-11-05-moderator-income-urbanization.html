--- layout: post title: "Does income change the link between urbanization and life expectancy? Testing a moderator" date: 2026-11-05 09:30:00 +0500 ---

This is the last post in a small series on the Gapminder country data from the Data Analysis Tools course (213 countries, one row per country). The previous post found a correlation of r = 0.61 between the urban share of the population and life expectancy. Here I ask whether that relationship is the same for poor, middle-income and rich countries.

Question and variables

Question: does the correlation between urbanization and life expectancy depend on a country's income group?

  • Explanatory variable: urbanrate, urban population as a percentage of the total.
  • Response variable: lifeexpectancy, life expectancy at birth in years.
  • Moderator (third variable): income group, made by cutting incomeperperson into three equal-sized groups (tertiles): low, middle, high.

Method. The test is a Pearson correlation. I run it once for all countries, then separately inside each income group, and compare. If the correlation looks clearly different between groups, income moderates the relationship. Significance level for each correlation: 0.05. In each group, H0 is that the correlation is 0.

Load and prepare the data

I keep the 173 countries that have all four series variables (income, life expectancy, internet use, urbanization), the same sample as in the other posts. The file is the Gapminder course file as hosted in the public GitHub repository raffg/Coursera-Data-Analysis-and-Interpretation.

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import scipy.stats as stats

data = pd.read_csv("data/gapminder.csv", na_values=" ")
cols = ["incomeperperson", "lifeexpectancy", "internetuserate", "urbanrate"]
data[cols] = data[cols].apply(pd.to_numeric, errors="coerce")
sub = data.dropna(subset=cols).copy()
sub["income_group"] = pd.qcut(sub["incomeperperson"], 3, labels=["low", "middle", "high"])
print("countries with all four variables:", len(sub))
print(sub["income_group"].value_counts().sort_index())

Output:

Overall correlation (no moderator)

r, p = stats.pearsonr(sub["urbanrate"], sub["lifeexpectancy"])
print(f"All countries: n = {len(sub)}, r = {r:.3f}, r squared = {r**2:.3f}, p = {p:.3g}")

Output:

All countries: n = 173, r = 0.615, r squared = 0.378, p = 2.25e-19

Correlation within each income group

for level in ["low", "middle", "high"]:
    g = sub[sub["income_group"] == level]
    r, p = stats.pearsonr(g["urbanrate"], g["lifeexpectancy"])
    print(f"{level:>6} income: n = {len(g)}, r = {r:.3f}, r squared = {r**2:.3f}, p = {p:.3g}")

Output:

Interpretation

Overall, urbanization and life expectancy are clearly related (r = 0.615, p < 0.001). Once the countries are split by income, the picture changes:

  • Low income: r = 0.172, p = 0.197. Not significant, so I cannot say there is a relationship in this group.
  • Middle income: r = 0.080, p = 0.555. Not significant, and r squared is only 0.6%.
  • High income: r = 0.437, p = 0.0006. Significant: more urban rich countries tend to live longer, and urbanization describes about 19% of the variation in life expectancy.

So the relationship is not the same at every income level. Most of the overall correlation seems to come from rich countries being both highly urban and long-lived, and from poor countries being less urban and shorter-lived, which is income showing through. Within the low and middle groups, urban share by itself tells us little about life expectancy. This is evidence that income moderates (or, more carefully, accounts for much of) the relationship.

Limits. I compared the three correlations by eye and by their p-values; I did not run a formal test of whether the correlations differ from each other, and a significant result in one group and a non-significant one in another is not by itself proof that they differ. The groups have 57 to 58 countries each, so smaller correlations could be missed. The high-income scatter also contains two unusual points (one country with only about 13 percent urban population, one with life expectancy near 51) that affect that group's r.
